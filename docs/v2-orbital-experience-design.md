# Scroll-driven orbital experience

The approved reference puts a large, detailed planet beside a readable experience timeline, with a wide heading, quiet controls and open space. The current `/resume` implementation follows that composition and makes page scrolling drive the journey.

## Behavior

- Earth appears automatically; the remaining worlds preload after the first scene. Two original spacecraft—an optical observatory and a dish-equipped relay—orbit on different inclinations and slightly eccentric paths. They have articulated photovoltaic wings, cell-level textures, thermal foil, radiators and instruments. Nadir pointing is computed in the local orbital frame; Earth and its satellites share the local-time sunlight. Their size is deliberately exaggerated for readability.
- Native page scrolling drives Earth → Moon → Mars → Jupiter → Saturn. The camera pulls back, brings both worlds into view, then approaches the next one. Reverse scrolling reverses that path.
- Journey positions derive from the actual timeline. Buttons jump to the corresponding scroll position. These worlds illustrate exploration; they do not represent factual places of employment or education.
- Moon and Mars settlements are attached to tangent frames on the actual globes. On arrival, the camera descends continuously from orbit; the planet remains opaque and the local ground curves to meet it. The original models use an illustrated scale so their skyline becomes visible during approach. Moon has pressure habitats, raised residential rings, gardens, science towers, elevated transit, cargo berths and an electromagnetic launchport. Mars adds a larger biosphere district, atmospheric processing, a transport harbour and a Starship-inspired vehicle.
- Terrain stays smoothly shaded. The outer ground margin blends into the planet beneath it. Long structural beams are subdivided before being conformed to the surface, keeping the lunar rail, coil frames and spacecraft aligned.
- One playback clock drives a 36-second lunar sequence: ready 0–5s, rail acceleration 5–10s, coast and unfolding wings 10–19s, carriage return 19–24s, then ready until the next cycle. The camera follows the departure before touring habitats and returning to the rail. This is an illustrative compressed sequence, not an orbital simulation.
- Drag, arrow keys or +/- hand over to free exploration. The guided camera holds while the launch clock continues. Replay/Home starts the next safe cycle boundary and eases the camera back into its path. Moon/Mars entry resets the shared clock; hidden, offscreen, paused and reduced-motion states consume no time. The wheel always scrolls the page; mobile retains one-finger scrolling and two-finger orbit.
- Pause freezes the rendered camera, including during a descent or manual inertia. Explicit navigation while paused switches to a static destination. Reduced motion also uses static destinations.
- A small scroll window holds each chapter's view steady while reading. Free exploration freezes the journey where it was taken over. Scrolling resumes travel; scroll input during a drag waits until release. A later destination selection wins over that queued scroll. Surface departures first lift away from the current view, then move between planets. A reversal or Replay begins from the rendered pose.
- Desktop keeps the planet beside the record. Mobile begins with an automatic orbital view, then shrinks the sticky scene while the visitor reads. Explore expands it; Back to reading or Escape returns to the reading layout. The record, résumé links, chapter links and credentials remain readable without JavaScript.

## Rendering

`src/scripts/experience.ts` owns scrolling, lifecycle and control intent. `src/scripts/experience-camera.ts` alone writes the rendered camera: guided motion follows spherical paths with bounded translation and rotation, and enforces globe clearance before rendering. OrbitControls drives a separate input camera in a fixed local up frame, so its cached axis and distance limits cannot interfere with the guided descent. Pose telemetry supports browser regression checks; it is not displayed in the page.

`src/scripts/planet-materials.ts` owns planetary geometry and materials. Earth combines an 8K surface on suitable desktops, 4K cloud and night-light layers, a normal map, an ocean reflectance map, displaced cloud shadows and a thin atmospheric rim. Small, low-memory and data-saving devices use 2K Earth layers. Moon and Mars use 4K surface maps on desktop and 2K on smaller devices so the descent retains detail. Texture-size limits are checked. Pixel ratio is capped at 2. The near clipping plane follows camera distance: 0.05 in Earth orbit, smaller only for close surface views. This preserves depth separation between Earth’s surface and thin cloud layer. Resizing the canvas or loading fonts updates measurements without changing the selected destination; page scrolling still drives travel. Saturn retains textured rings and shadows.

`src/lib/scene-clock.ts` resolves the browser timezone in an inline head script on every page, starting on the visitor’s first page. It caches only the timezone in session storage for up to six hours, safely falls back when storage/Intl is unavailable, and derives the current phase from the current time. Earth waits for this result and a complete first 3D frame. The continuous Milky Way remains visible during loading; there is no placeholder Earth image. `src/lib/scene-time.ts` defines the local phase boundaries: dawn 06:00–08:59, day 09:00–16:59, dusk 17:00–19:59, night 20:00–05:59. Four authored lighting treatments change direction, warmth, the terminator and visible city lights. The page checks each minute and when a tab becomes visible. It makes no time-zone, geolocation or weather request. These are illustrative lighting treatments, not an astronomical ephemeris.

The full-page Milky Way is a perspective projection of ESO/S. Brunier's photographic celestial panorama. The source is projected with JavaScript, rather than rotating a rectangle with visible empty corners. There is one page-owned sky, including on mobile; the sticky panel has no separately positioned sky image. The record is masked only where it passes behind the transparent scene. Credit stays visible beside the scene. The foreground remains dark enough to read.

`src/scripts/orbital-satellites.ts` contains the original Earth satellite geometry, while `src/scripts/lunar-launch.ts` poses the lunar payload and carriage from the shared clock in `src/lib/journey-playback.ts`. `src/scripts/planetary-outposts.ts` loads compressed original Blender models, conforms their geometry to the globe, attaches them to planetary surface frames, and defines the district camera paths. `src/scripts/experience-layout.ts` coordinates mobile reading/expanded states and the record mask. Surface models share a bounded environment map and are disposed on exit, including embedded textures and Draco workers. Draco's local JavaScript decoder needs no extra evaluation privileges. `/resume` alone allows local blob fetches for embedded GLB images; other page policies stay unchanged.

The live Earth uses Three.js geometry. The previously rendered Earth posters are archived assets and are no longer requested by the experience page.

A failed initial load leaves the background, readable experience record, an explanatory status, and a retry button. If a later globe fails, the last available world remains visible and its selector can retry. A missing surface model leaves its globe usable. Rendering pauses when hidden or offscreen; graphics resources, timers and listeners are disposed on page exit. Pause and reduced motion also stop satellite, cloud and guided-camera movement. Ordinary articles load neither the renderer nor its maps or models.

## Reproduction and references

Run `node scripts/artwork/prepare-orbit-assets.mjs` for the new planet layers and sky. Run `node scripts/artwork/prepare-outpost-ground.mjs`, then `blender -b --python scripts/artwork/build-outposts.py` to export the two Draco-compressed GLBs. Adding `-- --render` makes GPU previews. `scripts/artwork/render-earth-posters.py` reproduces the archived four planet renders on CUDA; `scripts/artwork/encode-earth-posters.mjs` prepares their web deliveries.

The settlements are original, imagined future architecture informed by [NASA's Mars Surface Habitat concept study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230014593), [SpaceX's Mars program](https://www.spacex.com/mars), [O'Neill/SSI mass-driver research](https://nss.org/l5-news-mass-driver-update/) and [SpaceX's lunar manufacturing concept](https://www.spacex.com/updates). They are not official NASA/SpaceX designs or engineering plans. Ground material is a tinted adaptation of Poly Haven's CC0 [Rocks Ground 06](https://polyhaven.com/a/rocks_ground_06).

## Visual system

Manrope headings, DM Mono annotations, charcoal and cream, amber `#DFA05D`, brick `#AC5045`, and muted green `#658761`. Planet colors retain natural variation. The heading and timeline sit in the same composition as the visual, without a boxed control panel. World buttons are larger, Pause is an explicit text control, and detailed instructions stay in a disclosure. Projects opens with the real project cards directly after its introduction.

## Verification

Review actual desktop/mobile screenshots and forward/reverse scroll states, including the middle of a transition. Browser contracts cover automatic start, scroll pullback, reverse travel, manual controls, pause, reduced motion, one-finger page scrolling, context loss/retry, article isolation and no-JavaScript reading. Fixture runs explicitly select `tests/v2/browser/worker.env` so a developer's live CMS settings cannot replace the test data.

`camera-handoffs.spec.ts` records actual position, focus, up direction, quaternion and ground clearance through interrupted descents, manual/scroll handoffs, queued inputs and rapid reselection. Camera math tests also cover opposite-side approaches and isolation from OrbitControls' zoom clamps.
